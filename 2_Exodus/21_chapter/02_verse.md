# כִּ֤י תִקְנֶה֙ עֶ֣בֶד עִבְרִ֔י שֵׁ֥שׁ שָׁנִ֖ים יַעֲבֹ֑ד וּבַ֨שְּׁבִעִ֔ת יֵצֵ֥א לַֽחָפְשִׁ֖י חִנָּֽם׃

## Text
### Masoretic Notes
- **Accents.** The zaqef qatan on עִבְרִ֔י closes the case; the atnach on יַעֲבֹ֑ד divides the two rules, service and release. עֶ֣בֶד עִבְרִ֔י is joined by munach, which marks one phrase but cannot show whether it is noun + adjective or construct + genitive.
- **וּבַשְּׁבִעִת** is written without *yod* after the ב; BDB lists this spelling for this verse only. Minchat Shai records that in the manuscripts he cites (בס"ס) the ב has no qadma; the Leningrad Codex has one.
- **Paragraphing.** The unit 21:1–11 is closed by a *setumah* after v. 6 and another after v. 11. This verse opens the first paragraph.

## Language
Counts are from the ETCBC BHSA database (complete for the word or construction searched; each case read by hand) unless marked otherwise.

### Words
**תִקְנֶה.** קָנָה with a person as object means "buy" (BDB sense 2), and the object is named by what the purchase makes him. Free Egyptians say קְנֵה־אֹתָנוּ… וְנִהְיֶה אֲנַחְנוּ… עֲבָדִים לְפַרְעֹה (Gen 47:19; cf. 47:23); מֵהֶם תִּקְנוּ עֶבֶד וְאָמָה (Lev 25:44); קָנִיתִי עֲבָדִים וּשְׁפָחוֹת (Eccl 2:7); a man who sold himself has a קֹנֵהוּ (Lev 25:50; cf. Deut 28:68). "Buy a slave" therefore does not imply that he was a slave before the sale, against Gur Aryeh (לשון עבד עברי משמע שהוא עבד כבר), Hirsch, and the view Kli Yakar reports (יֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים).

**עֶבֶד עִבְרִי.** "A slave who is a Hebrew." עֶבֶד has the same form in construct and absolute (Mizrachi), so "a Hebrew's slave" is possible in form, and MdRY Nezikin 1:8–9 raises that reading before rejecting it. Usage excludes it: every other noun + עִבְרִי is noun + adjective, including הָעֶבֶד הָעִבְרִי (Gen 39:17), with the article on both words; so too אִישׁ עִבְרִי (Gen 39:14; Ex 2:11), נַעַר עִבְרִי (Gen 41:12), אָחִיךָ הָעִבְרִי (Deut 15:12; Jer 34:14). Reggio: עברי תואר לעבד לא נסמך אליו.

**עִבְרִי** occurs 34 times (masculine and feminine):
- Israelites or their ancestors, set against Egyptians or Philistines (19): Gen 39:14, 17; 40:15; 41:12; 43:32; Ex 1:15, 16, 19; 2:6, 7, 11, 13; 1 Sam 4:6, 9; 13:3, 7, 19; 14:11; 29:3
- "the God of the Hebrews," said to Pharaoh (6): Ex 3:18; 5:3; 7:16; 9:1, 13; 10:3
- Jonah's answer to "what is your people?" (1): עִבְרִי אָנֹכִי (Jonah 1:8–9)
- the slave laws (6): Ex 21:2; Deut 15:12 (×2); Jer 34:9 (×2), 14. Jer 34:9 glosses the phrase: בִּיהוּדִי אָחִיהוּ
- Abram (1): אַבְרָם הָעִבְרִי (Gen 14:13)
- 1 Sam 14:21 (1), the only verse that sets "the Hebrews" apart from Israel: וְהָעִבְרִים הָיוּ לַפְּלִשְׁתִּים… וְגַם־הֵמָּה לִהְיוֹת עִם־יִשְׂרָאֵל

No occurrence denotes an Ishmaelite, Edomite or descendant of Keturah. Ibn Ezra made the count: הנה בכל המקרא לא מצאנו מלת עברי כי אם על בני יעקב. Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan translate עַבְדָא בַר יִשְׂרָאֵל.

**יִשְׂרְאֵלִי** occurs 5 times (Lev 24:10 ×3, 24:11; 2 Sam 17:25). Hebrew had almost no other word than עִבְרִי for an individual Israelite set against foreigners, so the choice of "Hebrew" over "Israel" supports no inference. That applies to the Netziv (דמסתמא הוא אדם זול ביותר ואין עליו אלא שם עברי), to Malbim (the name Israel would exclude converts) and to Kli Yakar (he is called עִבְרִי because he returned to the ways of Abraham's forefathers, and because he is an עוֹבֵר עֲבֵרָה).

**יַעֲבֹד.** No object suffix. The same service to the same master is told with and without one: אֶעֱבָדְךָ שֶׁבַע שָׁנִים בְּרָחֵל (Gen 29:18); וַיַּעֲבֹד יַעֲקֹב בְּרָחֵל שֶׁבַע שָׁנִים (29:20); עֲבַדְתִּיךָ… וְשֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים בְּצֹאנֶךָ (31:41). Here the master is the "you" of the case and the rule speaks of the slave in the third person, so no object is needed. Deuteronomy's own rule has one: וַעֲבָדְךָ שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים (15:12; cf. 15:18). The LXX, the Vulgate and Rasag all supply "you."

**וּבַשְּׁבִעִת.** A feminine ordinal with no שָׁנָה. In the Torah's laws, שָׁנָה + ordinal always denotes a calendar year counted from Tishrei (Lev 19:24, 25; 25:4, 20, 21, 22 ×2; Deut 26:12: 8 cases), except Deut 15:12, which the halacha counts from the sale. A bare ordinal denoting a year occurs twice in the Torah: here, and Ex 23:11, וְהַשְּׁבִיעִת תִּשְׁמְטֶנָּה, which is the sabbatical year. (Pattern search over ordinals, read by hand.) So the missing שָׁנָה does not decide the count by itself; see Midrash and Talmud below.

**יֵצֵא לַחָפְשִׁי.**
- **יָצָא** makes the release the slave's own act, as in vv. 3–5 and v. 11. Deuteronomy and Jeremiah make it the master's: תְּשַׁלְּחֶנּוּ חָפְשִׁי (Deut 15:12, 13, 18); וְשִׁלַּחְתּוֹ חָפְשִׁי (Jer 34:14). The tooth-and-eye release also takes שִׁלַּח (Ex 21:26–27).
- **חָפְשִׁי** occurs 17 times. Only three have ל + article: Ex 21:2, 26, 27. The same release is plain חָפְשִׁי in 21:5 (לֹא אֵצֵא חָפְשִׁי), Deut 15:12–18 and Jer 34:9–16.
- **The ל of לַחָפְשִׁי.** Ibn Ezra reports R. Marinus (the *yod* is added, as in לְמִשְׁעִי) and R. Yehudah (the ל is added), and himself says two added letters stand where one would do (שנים משרתים והאחד היה מספיק). Rashbam argues from the *patach* that the word is a noun, "to freedom"; Rashi and Reggio: לְחֵרוּת. Shadal: a feminine noun of Aramaic type, like נְוָלִי. All the ancient translations render an adjective, "free."
- Nothing legal turns on the form. Hoffmann says the Talmud distinguishes חפשי from לחפשי (Kiddushin 24b), but R. Meir's argument there is from word order in vv. 26–27: אִי כְּתַב "חׇפְשִׁי" לְבַסּוֹף… הַשְׁתָּא דִּכְתַב "לַחׇפְשִׁי יְשַׁלְּחֶנּוּ" – הֲוָה לֵיהּ חׇפְשִׁי מֵעִיקָּרָא.

**חִנָּם** occurs 32 times, in three senses (BDB): without payment, without cause (1 Sam 19:5; Ps 35:7, 19; Job 2:3), in vain (Ezek 6:10; Mal 1:10; Prov 1:17). Where labour or goods are at issue it means "without payment": הֲכִי־אָחִי אַתָּה וַעֲבַדְתַּנִי חִנָּם (Gen 29:15); בְּרֵעֵהוּ יַעֲבֹד חִנָּם וּפֹעֲלוֹ לֹא יִתֶּן־לוֹ (Jer 22:13); עֹלוֹת חִנָּם against בִּמְחִיר (2 Sam 24:24); חִנָּם נִמְכַּרְתֶּם וְלֹא בְכֶסֶף תִּגָּאֵלוּ (Isa 52:3); Ex 21:11. The Netziv holds that משמעות חנם בלשון תורה אינו מוחלט על העדר תשלומין, citing Num 11:5 as "free of commandments." That sense of Num 11:5 is the Sifrei's derash, which sets the plain sense aside on historical grounds (Rashi ad loc.: אִם תֶּבֶן לֹא הָיוּ נוֹתְנִין לָהֶם חִנָּם, דָּגִים הָיוּ נוֹתְנִין לָהֶם חִנָּם?). The plain sense there is also "for nothing."

### Grammar and Syntax
**Clauses** (BHSA):
1. [Conj כִּי] [Pred תִקְנֶה] [Objc עֶבֶד עִבְרִי], with עִבְרִי an adjective
2. [Time שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים] [Pred יַעֲבֹד]
3. [Conj וּ] [Time בַּשְּׁבִעִת] [Pred יֵצֵא] [Adju לַחָפְשִׁי] [Modi חִנָּם]

One case and two rules, each rule with its time phrase first, divided at the atnach.

**כִּי + second-person *yiqtol* states a case, not a command.** So Ex 23:4, 5; Deut 20:10; 21:10; 22:8; 23:22; 24:10, 19–21. Deut 23:22–23 says outright that such a case need not arise: כִּי־תִדֹּר נֶדֶר… וְכִי תֶחְדַּל לִנְדֹּר לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ חֵטְא. Ex 21:2 is the only second-person case among the 17 כִּי-cases of 21:2–22:16. The Sifrei's "when you buy, buy only a Hebrew slave" (below) is therefore not in the grammar; Lekach Tov makes the point: כי תקנה, לכשתקנה, כמו כי יהיה זב… ולא הוא מצוה עליהם שתקנה עבד עברי.

**A number before the verb is not marked as exact.** Malbim (*Ayelet HaShachar* §78; on 21:2) and Torah Temimah (משום דאל"ה הול"ל ועבד שש שנים) derive the runaway's duty to make up lost time from the placing of שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים before יַעֲבֹד. The rule fails:
- **Word order of durations in the Torah** (phrases with a cardinal and day/month/year; dates excluded):

  | Verb form | Number before the verb | Number after the verb |
  |---|---|---|
  | *yiqtol* | 32 | 13 |
  | *weqatal* | 0 | 24 |
  | *wayyiqtol* | 0 | 59 |

- **The same period takes both orders:** Ex 13:6 שִׁבְעַת יָמִים תֹּאכַל מַצֹּת / 13:7 מַצּוֹת יֵאָכֵל אֵת שִׁבְעַת הַיָּמִים; Deut 16:3 / 16:4; and in one verse, Lev 8:33 לֹא תֵצְאוּ שִׁבְעַת יָמִים… כִּי שִׁבְעַת יָמִים יְמַלֵּא אֶת־יֶדְכֶם.
- **The "six… and the seventh" formula always puts the six first** when its verb is a *yiqtol* (12 of 12: Ex 16:26; 20:9; 21:2; 23:10, 12; 31:15; 34:21; 35:2; Lev 23:3; 25:3; Deut 5:13; 16:8). The fronting sets the six against the seventh.
- **Deut 15:12 offers no contrast.** Its וַעֲבָדְךָ שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים is a *weqatal*, which must stand first in its clause.
- **The halacha itself counts a fronted term by the calendar:** orlah's שָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים יִהְיֶה לָכֶם עֲרֵלִים (Lev 19:23) runs from Tishrei, and thirty days before Rosh Hashanah count as a year (Rosh Hashanah 9b–10a).

The runaway rule stands on the baraita's reading of "he shall serve six years" as six years of service (Talmud, below).

**The niphal of מָכַר does not mean "sold by others."** Chizkuni, Da'at Zekenim and Hadar Zekenim read Deut 15:12's כִּי יִמָּכֵר as a sale by others, hence by the court. The halacha reads the same niphal of a man who sells himself: וְנִמְכַּר־לָךְ (Lev 25:39); וְנִמְכַּר לְגֵר תּוֹשָׁב (25:47); מִשְּׁנַת הִמָּכְרוֹ לוֹ (25:50). It is also used of sale by others (Ex 22:2; Neh 5:8). The explicit reflexive is the hitpael, וְהִתְמַכַּרְתֶּם (Deut 28:68). The Riva makes the point from Lev 25.

*Greek text: Brenton (1851), public domain. Rahlfs (1935) has the same wording.*

*Clementine Vulgate.*

- **serviet tibi**: "you" supplied, as in the LXX and Rasag (פליכ̇דמך).
- **in septimo**: no "year," as in the Hebrew.

## Structure
- **The main case of the first paragraph.** One כִּי followed by four אִם-sub-cases (vv. 3a, 3b, 4, 5). The second paragraph has the same shape: וְכִי (v. 7) and four (vv. 8, 9, 10, 11). Cassuto counts ten clauses: מספר העוזר לזכרון, כמספר עשרת הדיברות. Hoffmann counts five laws in each paragraph, citing Bertheau, who counts seven groups.
- **The code is framed by "six… and the seventh."** It opens its laws with the slave's six years and seventh (21:2) and near its end gives the land's and the week's (23:10–12), with the same bare ordinal (וּבַשְּׁבִעִת / וְהַשְּׁבִיעִת). The tradition ties the six years to the week of creation: Shemot Rabbah 30:5; Midrash Tannaim on Deut 15:12; Ramban; Rabbeinu Bahya; Kli Yakar (who prefers this reason: וְזֶה יוֹתֵר נָכוֹן); Abarbanel: כי אין המשפט הזה מהדינים בהחלט אבל יש לו סוד עמוק בחדוש הבריאה.
- **The only sale of a man that the code itself describes is for theft**: וְנִמְכַּר בִּגְנֵבָתוֹ ([22:2](../22_chapter/02_verse.md)). Rashbam and Abarbanel read v. 2 in its light, and Pseudo-Jonathan writes it into the translation (Midrash, below).
- **Why the code opens with the slave.**
  - Ibn Ezra: אין לאדם בעולם יותר קשה עליו מהיותו ברשות אדם כמוהו, על כן החל משפט העבד.
  - Abarbanel: the laws unfold the last commandments, and the first form of killing under לא תרצח is lifelong enslavement, כי זו היא רציחה בחיים. He cites Jer 34:17 as the measure-for-measure punishment.
  - Kli Yakar: the Decalogue opens with אֲשֶׁר הוֹצֵאתִיךָ… מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, so the laws open with the release of a slave.
  - Hirsch: the code opens with the thief, the criminal whom other legal systems punish most harshly in body and freedom.
- **Why six.** Kli Yakar reports that some take six as twice a hireling's three years (Isa 16:14 בְּשָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים כִּשְׁנֵי שָׂכִיר, with Deut 15:18 מִשְׁנֶה שְׂכַר שָׂכִיר), and the objection: Isa 21:16 puts כִּשְׁנֵי שָׂכִיר on a single year, so the phrase means "exactly counted," not "three."

## Rabbinic Interpretation
### Midrash
**Whom the verse covers.**
- **The thief sold by the court.** MdRY Nezikin 1:7. The Mekhilta reaches it by dividing the three passages, not from the words of this verse: since Lev 25:39 covers the self-seller, כְּשֶׁהוּא אוֹמֵר "וְכִי יָמוּךְ אָחִיךָ… וְנִמְכַּר לָךְ", הֲרֵי מוֹכֵר עַצְמוֹ אָמוּר. Pseudo-Jonathan translates the restriction into the verse: אֲרוּם תִּיזְבוּן בִּגְנֵיבוּתֵיהּ לְעַבְדָא בַּר יִשְרָאֵל. Rashbam, Rabbeinu Bahya and Abarbanel connect the verse to 22:2.
- **Both the thief and the self-seller.** A bracketed passage in MdRShbY 21:2: כי תקנה עבד עברי זה שהוא מוכר את עצמו… בא הכתוב ללמדך לשון קצרה כי תקנה עבד עבד בין שמוכר את עצמו ובין שבית דין מוכרין אותו לך. MdRShbY also reads the verse as buying the man from himself: כי תקנה עבד עברי. אתה קונה מעצמו.
- **The plain sense, on the testimony of commentators who follow the halacha.** Ibn Ezra's short commentary: מיד ב"ד שמכרוהו בגנבתו, או בעצמו… לולי הקבלה. Shadal: בכל אופן שיהיה, בין מוכר עצמו ובין מכרוהו ב"ד… כן נראה פשט הכתוב. Hoffmann: דומה שמוטב שלא להגביל את המלים "כי תקנה עבד עברי" למקרה מסוים.
- *Assessment.* Not in the words. The verse names no seller; "buy a slave" names the result of the purchase (Words); Deuteronomy's niphal does not mean a sale by others (Grammar). The restriction is a reading of the three passages as one statute, and on those terms it is coherent (Talmud, below).

**The three passages as one statute.** Sifra, Behar, Chapter 7:1, with near-identical wording in Sifrei Devarim 118:4 and MdRShbY 21:2, assigns one clause of each passage to one rule:

> מניין כשאתה קונה לא תהא קונה אלא עבד עברי? ת"ל (שמות כא ב) כי תקנה עבד עברי. מניין כשהוא נמכר אינו נמכר אלא לך? ת"ל (ויקרא כה לט) ונמכר לך. מניין כשבית דין מוכרים אותו אין מוכרים אותו אלא לך? ת"ל (דברים טו יב) כי ימכר לך.

The first clause states a preference for Hebrew over foreign slaves; Torah Temimah links it to Rambam, Mattenot Aniyim 9:17. *Assessment:* not in the grammar (see Grammar and Syntax); Lev 25:44 permits foreign slaves without qualification.

**"Hebrew."** MdRY Nezikin 1:8–9 rejects "the slave of a Hebrew" by *gezeirah shavah* with Deut 15:12, and labels its proof texts זֵכֶר לַדָּבָר, not proof. MdRShbY 21:2 gives three reasons for the name: the merit of Eber, Abraham who came מעבר הנהר, and Israel who spoke Hebrew. The Karaites read עֶבֶד עִבְרִי as any descendant of Abraham (Ibn Ezra; Hoffmann, reporting the *Mivchar*). Ibn Ezra's refutation, repeated by Malbim: ועוד איך יעלה על לב אדם כי הישמעאלי והאדומי יעבוד שש שנים, והישראלי עד שנת היובל. *Assessment:* "an Israelite slave" is the sense of the words (Words).

**Serves the son, not other heirs.** Sifrei Devarim 118:6 presents this as a chosen middle, with a reason (the son stands in his father's place), not as a reading: מִי לְחָשְׁךָ לְהָבִיא אֶת הַבֵּן וּלְהוֹצִיא אֶת הַיּוֹרֵשׁ? Malbim grounds it in the suffix: יַעֲבֹד without one here includes heirs, וַעֲבָדְךָ with one in Deut 15:12 excludes them, so the son alone is included. *Assessment:* the suffix contrast is not real (Words, יַעֲבֹד). The rule rests on the Sifrei's reasoning.

**Six years of service: the runaway and the sick slave.** The runaway makes up lost time; the sick slave does not (Sifrei Devarim 118:7; Kiddushin 16b–17a; Y. Kiddushin 1:2). Malbim explains the difference from the verb: וכשחלה אף שאינו עובד בפועל הוא עבד ומוכן לעבוד, לא כן הבורח שהוציא א"ע מרשות האדון לגמרי. The Yerushalmi says the same: the verse includes the one who remains in the master's power and excludes the one who does not. *Assessment:* a sound reading of "serve" as the status of being at the master's disposal. Malbim's further ground, the number placed before the verb, fails (Grammar).

**The seventh: of the sale, or of the sabbatical cycle.** The halacha counts from the sale (MdRY Nezikin 1:16). The tannaitic sources show that the other reading was considered:
- **Sifra, Behar, Chapter 3:6** states the *a fortiori* for release at the sabbatical year and blocks it with one word of Lev 25:13:

  > "בשנת היובל הזאת" — זו מוציאה עבדים ואין שביעית מוציאה עבדים. והלא דין הוא! ומה אם היובל — שאינו משמט כספים — מוציא עבדים, שביעית — שמשמטת כספים — אינו דין שתוציא עבדים?! תלמוד לומר "בשנת היובל הזאת" — זו מוציאה עבדים ואין השביעית מוציאה עבדים.

- **MdRShbY 21:2** decides between release at the start or the end of the seventh year by classing it with the Jubilee, not with the sabbatical remission of debts: דנין דבר שתלוי ביובל מדבר שתלוי ביובל, ואל יוכיח השמט מלוה שאין תלוי ביובל.
- **Midrash Tannaim on Deut 15:12** raises the sabbatical reading on the verse that says בַּשָּׁנָה הַשְּׁבִיעִת, and answers only from the six years: וּבַשָּׁנָה הַשְּׁבִיעִית — שביעית למכירה. אתה אומר שביעית למכירה, או שביעית לשמטה? תלמוד לומר "שש שנים" — הוי אומר זו שביעית למכירה. Malbim (d. 1879) could not have seen this text, which Hoffmann published in 1908–09.
- **Pseudo-Jonathan** has only וּבְמַעֲלֵי שְׁבִיעָתָא, "at the entry of the seventh," here and at Deut 15:12. At 21:7 it has בִּשְׁנִין דִּשְׁמִיטָתָא, and at 22:2 the thief is sold וְעַד שַׁתָּא דִשְׁמִיטְתָא. Hoffmann calls this תמוה. It is the one ancient translation that ties the release to the sabbatical cycle.
- **Malbim's rule** (*Ayelet HaShachar* §84; on Lev 19:24): with the word שָׁנָה a year is a calendar year from Tishrei; without it, "his own year, from day to day." The rule holds for every legal instance with שָׁנָה except Deut 15:12 (Words), but Malbim himself raises the counterexample, Ex 23:11, and falls back on the Yerushalmi's argument from the Jubilee.
- *Assessment.* For Exodus the count from the sale is very likely: the bare ordinal follows "six years he shall serve," and the tradition has three arguments for it (Talmud, below). For Deut 15:12 the halacha rests on one of them, the argument from the six years.

**Free, for nothing.**
- **No payment, and no writ.** MdRY Nezikin 1:17 reads יֵצֵא against Deuteronomy's וְכִי תְשַׁלְּחֶנּוּ: the release happens without a writ. MdRShbY: שמצוה לומר [לו] צא… ת"ל יצא לחפשי חנם. MdRShbY also derives "no writ" by *gezeirah shavah* חפשי/חפשי with v. 26.
- **Not even medical costs.** MdRShbY 21:2: ד"א חנם — יכול אם היה חולה ומוטל במטה והוציא עליו רבו יציאות יהא צריך ליתן לו? ת"ל יצא לחפשי חנם. Rambam codifies it (Avadim 2:12). Torah Temimah, not knowing a source, reconstructs it from MdRY's bare חנם (ולא נתבאר מקור הדברים); Elon (*Jewish Law*, n. 390) notes that the source was unknown until MdRShbY was published.
- **Five modes of release.** MdRShbY lists שנים, יובל, גרעון כסף, שטר שחרור, ומיתת האדון בלא בן. The writ and the master's sonless death are not in Mishnah Kiddushin 1:2; Rambam has all five (Avadim 2:12).
- *Assessment.* Supported. Exodus makes the release the slave's act (Words, יָצָא). The halacha keeps both texts: a duty to send him (מִצְוָה לוֹמַר לוֹ צֵא) and a release that happens without it.

**Allegory.** MdRShbY 21:2 cites *dorshei reshumot*: שש שנים יעבד בבל ומדי ופרס ויון ואשור ורומי, and in the seventh Moses asks that Israel not sink under Rome.

### Talmud
**Two kinds of Hebrew slave** (Kiddushin 14b–15a). The court-sold thief and the self-seller are distinguished by a *gezeirah shavah* שָׂכִיר/שָׂכִיר (Lev 25:40; Deut 15:18). R. Elazar rejects the distinction. Rambam lists the differences (Avadim 3:12):

| | Sold by the court | Sold himself |
|---|---|---|
| Term | six years only | six or more |
| Ear pierced | yes | no |
| Given a Canaanite bondwoman | yes | no |
| Severance gift | yes | no |
| May be sold to a gentile | no | yes |

- **The Ritva** holds that the sugya's conclusion (Kiddushin 16a) does not rest on the שכיר/שכיר analogy.
- **Malbim** grounds the distinction in what Lev 25:39–43 omits: וכפי דרך הפשט כ"ז מבואר מפשט הכתוב — שבפ' כי ימוך לא נזכר עבודת שש… וכן לא נזכר שמוסר לו ש"כ… וכן לא נזכר שנרצע… וכן לא נזכר בו דין הענקה. He adds the positive marker in Lev 25:41, וְיָצָא מֵעִמָּךְ הוּא וּבָנָיו עִמּוֹ.
- **Torah Temimah** (on Lev 25:39) concedes that without the derashah one would have taken Exodus as the general law: דהו"א דדין שש שנים שבר"פ משפטים אכל המכירות קאי.
- *Assessment.* Weak as a reading of this verse; strong as an argument from the silences of the Leviticus passage.

**The self-seller's term.** The baraita המוכר עצמו נמכר לשש ויותר על שש (Kiddushin 14b). According to the Ritva, Rashi holds that his term is six years unless stated otherwise (סתמא לשש); Rashi on Lev 25:40 presupposes it (אם פגע בו יובל לפני שש שנים). The Ritva rejects it, with Tosafot: דהא לא אשכחן ליה דרשא דקרא שיהא סתמא לשש… ושלא כדברי רש"י ז"ל שפירש שסתמו לשש. Rambam: הַמּוֹכֵר עַצְמוֹ יֵשׁ לוֹ לִמְכֹּר עַצְמוֹ לְיֶתֶר עַל שֵׁשׁ (Avadim 2:3).

**The runaway.** Kiddushin 16b: מִנַּיִן לְבוֹרֵחַ שֶׁחַיָּיב לְהַשְׁלִים – תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר: ״שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים יַעֲבֹד״. Kiddushin 17a and Y. Kiddushin 1:2 discuss the sick slave; the Yerushalmi also discusses a slave who fell ill after fleeing.

**Serves the son.** Kiddushin 17b: כשהוא אומר "שש שנים יעבד" הרי לבן אמור.

**The six years run from the day of sale.** Arakhin 18b: שֵׁשׁ שֶׁבְּעֶבֶד עִבְרִי — דִּכְתִיב: ״שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים יַעֲבֹד וּבַשְּׁבִיעִית״, זִימְנִין דְּבִשְׁבִיעִית נָמֵי יַעֲבוֹד. A slave sold in Nisan serves into the seventh calendar year.

**Y. Kiddushin 1:2: the seventh of the sale or of the world.**

> וּבַשְּׁבִיעִית יֵצֵא — שְׁבִיעִית שֶׁלַּמְכִירָה, לֹא שְׁבִיעִית שֶׁלְּעוֹלָם. אַתָּה אוֹמֵר שְׁבִיעִית שֶׁלַּמְכִירָה, אוֹ אֵינָהּ אֶלָּא שְׁבִיעִית שֶׁלְּעוֹלָם?

- The first answer, from the six years, is met with אֵימָא חֲלִיף, "say the reverse."
- R. Ze'ira in the name of Rav Huna argues from the wording: ובשביעית כתיב.
- R. Huna argues from the system: אִם אוֹמֵר אַתְּ שְׁבִיעִית שֶׁלְּעוֹלָם, אִם כֵּן מָה הַיּוֹבֵל בָּא וּמוֹצִיא? The argument holds only if Exodus and Leviticus 25 are one system. Malbim notes that it also depends on the dispute at Nedarim 61b over whether the Jubilee year counts in the sabbatical cycle.
- **Text.** In the printed (Leiden) text the rabbis of Caesarea conclude: שְׁבִיעִית מוֹצִיאָה אֶת הָעֲבָדִים וְיוֹבֵל אֶת הַנִּרְצָעִים. Malbim and the classical commentators read the reverse; Guggenheimer rejects the emendation on manuscript evidence. If the printed text is right, it is an amoraic statement that the sabbatical year frees slaves. The question needs the manuscripts.
- Torah Temimah notes the practical consequence of "the start of the seventh" rather than "the end of the sixth": in a leap year a term ending at the close of Adar I runs to the end of Adar II.

**The law applies only while the Jubilee is in force.** Arakhin 29a, 32b–33a; Tractate Avadim 1:1. Guggenheimer, on Y. Kiddushin 1:2, calls the male-slave discussion "a purely theoretical reconstruction of the past."

## Halacha
- **Mishnah Kiddushin 1:2**: עֶבֶד עִבְרִי נִקְנֶה בְכֶסֶף וּבִשְׁטָר, וְקוֹנֶה אֶת עַצְמוֹ בַּשָּׁנִים וּבַיּוֹבֵל וּבְגִרְעוֹן כֶּסֶף.
- **Talmud**: Kiddushin 14b–17b (the two categories, the runaway and the sick slave, service to the son); Arakhin 18b (the six years run from the day of sale).
- **Rambam, Hilkhot Avadim**:
  - **1:1** The Hebrew slave is either sold by the court or sells himself. וְאֵין לְךָ אִישׁ בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל שֶׁמּוֹכְרִין אוֹתוֹ בֵּית דִּין אֶלָּא הַגַּנָּב בִּלְבַד. וְעַל זֶה שֶׁמְּכָרוּהוּ בֵּית דִּין הוּא אוֹמֵר (שמות כא ב) "כִּי תִקְנֶה עֶבֶד עִבְרִי". A man may sell himself only when nothing is left to him, not even clothing, and only to buy food.
  - **1:10** Neither the Hebrew slave nor the Hebrew maidservant applies except while the Jubilee is in force.
  - **2:2** He serves six years from the day of sale and goes free at the start of his seventh year. If a sabbatical year falls within the six, he works through it; if the Jubilee falls, he goes free, even one year after the sale.
  - **2:3** A self-seller may sell himself for more than six years.
  - **2:4–5** A runaway, whether court-sold or self-sold, makes up the six years. Illness totalling less than four years counts toward the six; four years or more must be made up.
  - **2:12** He serves the master's son but no other heir. The five modes of release. מִצְוָה לוֹמַר לוֹ צֵא בִּשְׁעַת יְצִיאָתוֹ. אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁלֹּא אָמַר לוֹ הֲרֵי הוּא יוֹצֵא בְּחִנָּם. וְאֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ שְׁטָר. Even the master's medical expenses are not owed.
  - **3:12** The differences between the court-sold slave and the self-seller.
- **Sefer HaMitzvot**, Positive 232; **Sefer HaChinukh** 42.
- **Shulchan Arukh**: not codified; the institution lapsed with the Jubilee.
- *Not yet checked*: the Geonic sources (Sheiltot, Halakhot Gedolot, Rif on Kiddushin).

## Modern Scholarship
### Source Analysis
**How the three slave laws relate** (Ex 21:2–11; Lev 25:39–55; Deut 15:12–18; table under Cross-References).
- **Complementary.** Aaron Koller, ["The Hebrew Slave: Reading the Law Collections as Complementary"](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-hebrew-slave-reading-the-law-collections-as-complementary) (TheTorah.com), reads Lev 25:25–54 as "a linear progression" of one farmer's decline, and gives a reason for its Jubilee term: released after six years, the man "would be in precisely the same situation he was in six years earlier," since his land returns only at the Jubilee. He proposes, with the Mekhilta, that Exodus and Deuteronomy "may be discussing" a man sold by the court for theft (Ex 22:1–2), and that Deuteronomy's inclusion of women fills a gap in Exodus rather than contradicting it.
- **Developmental.** Zev Farber, ["The Hebrew Slave: Exodus, Leviticus, and Deuteronomy"](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-hebrew-slave-exodus-leviticus-and-deuteronomy) (TheTorah.com): Deuteronomy rewrites Exodus ("Hebrew slave" becomes "your brother"; nothing becomes a severance gift; the piercing loses אֶל־הָאֱלֹהִים because worship is centralised). He reports that several scholars take Deuteronomy to abolish Ex 21:7–11. The debt-release cycle of Deut 15:1–11 suggests that its seventh year for slaves is the sabbatical year. The slave laws "have not been brought together in the Torah in a single place, and presented as cases and sub-cases, as we might expect in a single-authored text." Leviticus "more or less abolishes slavery" while "extending their period of servitude."
- **Leviticus as a different case.** As reported by Harbin (*Tyndale Bulletin* 63.1, 2012, open access): Chirichigno (*Debt-Slavery*, 1993, p. 353) distinguishes the Lev 25 service from the other two, and calls it "not really debt-slavery at all" (p. 336); Baker (*Tight Fists or Open Hands?*, 2009, pp. 170–73) holds that Leviticus addresses a landholder and Exodus and Deuteronomy a landless man; Milgrom (*Leviticus 23–27*, 2001, p. 2163): "The Israelite can, therefore, never become a slave."
- **Order of the corpora.** Japhet (*Scripta Hierosolymitana* 31, 1986) and Milgrom place Leviticus 25 before Deuteronomy 15 (as reported). See also Levinson, "The Manumission of Hermeneutics" (VTSup 109, 2006; free on SSRN), and Schenker, *JSOT* 78 (1998).

**The sabbatical-year reading.** Farber's reading of Deut 15:12 as release at the sabbatical year is raised in the tannaitic sources themselves (Midrash Tannaim on Deut 15:12; Sifra, Behar, Chapter 3:6; Y. Kiddushin 1:2; see Midrash and Talmud). Pseudo-Jonathan at 21:7 and 22:2 reads it.

### Ancient Near Eastern Context
- **Hammurabi §117.** A debtor's wife, son or daughter sold or given for debt serves three years and is freed in the fourth (King's 1910 translation also has the debtor selling "himself"; not yet checked against Roth). Cassuto: §117 corresponds to the daughter's sale of vv. 7–11, not to v. 2.
- ***Ḫabiru / ʿapiru*.** Cassuto reads עִבְרִי in this law as a social class, cognate with Akkadian *ḫabiru*, attested at Nuzi for foreign dependants serving as slaves. The law then tells Israel to treat such slaves with mercy, יהיה הגזע שלהם מה שיהיה. He grants that by Jeremiah's time the term meant a Judean slave (Jer 34:9). BDB records the proposed link. Inside the Bible only 1 Sam 14:21 could support a class sense (Words). Not yet read at first hand.
- **Complementary collections.** Koller compares the Laws of Eshnunna §§53–54 with Hammurabi §251, where a later collection covers a case an earlier one left open.

**Jeremiah 34:8–22**, the only narrative of this law. Zedekiah's covenant to free Hebrew slaves, male and female, is made and broken during the siege.
- **v. 9** glosses עֶבֶד עִבְרִי as בִּיהוּדִי אָחִיהוּ.
- **v. 14** quotes Deut 15:12, including both מִקֵּץ שֶׁבַע שָׁנִים and וַעֲבָדְךָ שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים. Rav Nachman b. Yitzchak (Arakhin 33a) reads them as two slaves: the ordinary slave after six years, and the pierced slave at the Jubilee. Malbim and Abarbanel read the seventh year of service.
- **Whom the release covered.** Hirsch argues that the chapter supports Rashi's view that a self-seller is sold for six years by default. The attribution to Rashi is correct (Ritva on Kiddushin 14b; Rashi on Lev 25:40). The support comes from the circumstances, not the wording: v. 14's wording is a quotation of Deuteronomy. A besieged city's slaves were debtors, not thieves, and the covenant covered women (v. 9), whom a court never sells. (Hirsch's English on Lev 25:39 reverses the Ritva's position; his note on Ex 21:6 states it correctly.)
- **Cassuto (on 21:1)**: the re-enslavement was evidently lawful, and a covenant between the halves of a calf would not have been needed for a royal decree, so the Torah's law was a religious obligation, not state law.
- **Y. Rosh Hashanah 3:5**: R. Samuel b. R. Isaac makes this chapter the commandment given at Ex 6:13; R. Ila says Israel was punished only for it, citing Jer 34:14; R. Nechemiah: words of Torah are poor in their place and rich in another.
- **Abarbanel** reads v. 17 as punishment for the enslavement itself, and vv. 18–20 for breaking the covenant.
